Backstory

Thank you for taking part in Personality Deducer. Here is what it was really about.

The short version

Everyone got the exact same “personal” profile. No AI read your answers. We wanted to see whether a one-size-fits-all description still feels like it was written just for you.

The original experiment (1949)

A psychologist named Bertram Forer gave his 39 students a personality test. A week later he handed each of them a “personal” result. Secretly, every result was the same 13 sentences, mostly copied from a horoscope book.

The students rated how accurate it was. Almost all of them, 38 out of 39, gave it a 4 or 5 out of 5.

Why it works

Sentences like “you can be critical of yourself” or “sometimes you’re outgoing, sometimes you’re reserved” are true of almost everyone. But when we believe something was written about us, we notice the parts that fit and skip the parts that don’t. This is called the Forer effect (also known as the Barnum effect). It’s why horoscopes and online personality quizzes can feel so accurate.

What our study adds

In 1949, the result seemed to come from a psychologist. We wanted to know if people trust the same generic profile just as much, or even more, when they are told an AI wrote it.

What we recorded

Your questionnaire answers, your two ratings, and whether you marked each of the 13 statements true, false, or unsure. These are linked only to your access code, not to your name.

What your ratings mean

Nothing bad about you. If the profile felt accurate, you reacted the way nearly everyone does, and that is exactly what this study is about.
